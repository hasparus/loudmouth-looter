# Five doom lines — cover premise

Current draft (PLAN.md) is six lines, not five:

> You are Hunters bound by different reasons.
> You face impossible things.
> You will change.
> You might be consumed.
> Slay the dragon.
> Or become something worse.

"You face impossible things" is the weak line — could be on any game's cover.

---

The draft never mentions fire, and fire is the whole mechanical language of the game: Pyre, Stoke the Fire, Jump Into Flames, Turn to Ash. Doom lines with no burning feel off-brand.

---

The title is a question, and the game has the answer as a literal list — the Goals. The premise can answer the cover:

> Vengeance. Power. Greed. Glory. Knowledge. An oath.
> Whatever brought you here, it burns.
> Feed it, and it carries you.
> Feed it too much, and it consumes you.
> Slay the dragon. Or become one.

---

Alternative: end on the mirror question, stealing the DM agenda line ("learn what they're willing to burn"):

> You are Hunters.
> Each of you has a reason.
> Every reason is kindling.
> Slay the dragon, or become one.
> What are you willing to burn?

Risk: two questions on one cover.

---

Mythic-exemplar direction (user, verbatim):

> Fall fearless like Beowulf.
> Let your wants change you like Fáfnir.
> Save a princess and blackmail townsfolk like Saint George.
> Slay the beast. Become the beast.
> Betray your friends. Give up.

---

"Slay the beast. Become the beast." — the mirrored repetition beats "or become one" and "or become something worse". Two swings of the same axe. Fáfnir was a dwarf; greed made him a dragon — the game has an exact answer, so the cover shouldn't be coy.

---

"Give up." as the final line of a doom broadside — and in this game giving up is the redemption move (Relinquish Your Goal). The cover's bleakest imperative is secretly the kindest rule. The person who's played will reread the cover differently.

---

Beowulf adds a fourth outcome no earlier draft had: slay the dragon AND die. Fall *fearless* — dying well is on the menu, not just winning or corruption.

---

Saint George "blackmail" — the legend really is a protection racket: he subdues the dragon, then offers to kill it if the town converts. Funniest line of the set, but PLAN.md says "no jokes" on the cover. Is it a joke, or just an unflattering truth about saints?

---

Verdict so far: the mythic-imperative direction wins over the goal-list and mirror-question drafts, but it's not final form yet.

---

"Beast" is deliberate. Everywhere else in the game it's "The Dragon" — capital T, capital D, named. The cover premise alone says "the beast": the title names it, the premise refuses to. Becoming "the beast" is also wider than becoming a dragon — dracolich servant, corrupted villain, whatever shape your fall takes.

---

Shape decision: the exemplar lines end on Saint George, then one final sentence in a different register closes the premise. George last means the sequence ends on the sourest myth — the "hero" who ran a protection racket — and the final line answers it without a "like".

---

The final sentence is a zoom-out to the table (user): "All that in 2–4 hours."

Myth, myth, myth — then suddenly the voice of a con-slot flyer. The register drop IS the joke, deadpan, no wink needed. It answers the second question a browsing stranger has ("why would I sit down?") right after the myths answer the first ("why would you fight?").

---

Zoom-out variant (user): "A roleplaying game for up to 5 people and 1 to 4 hours."

More informative — player count is real table-planning data. But it's back-cover spec voice; loses the deixis ("All that…") that pointed at the myths and made the drop land. Possible merge: "All that: a roleplaying game for up to five people and one to four hours."

Time range settled: 2–4 hours. (1-hour floor wasn't credible for a game with chargen and a long Aftermath; 2–4 reads honest, and honest is scarier.) The author runs it in under 2 — but he knows the rules. The printed range is for the stranger picking up the sheet, not the designer.

---

Shape locked: Beowulf → Fáfnir → Saint George → zoom-out. Four lines. The staccato block ("Slay the beast. Become the beast. Betray your friends. Give up.") is cut — Fáfnir's line now carries the whole transformation payload alone.

With the imperatives gone, the premise never says "slay the dragon" at all. The title and the woodcut carry the dragon; the premise only shows you three ways to be a hero and one price tag.

> Fall fearless like Beowulf.
> Let your wants change you like Fáfnir.

---

Fáfnir line settled: "Let your wants change you like Fáfnir" stays (user likes it). V1 copy confirmed; V2/V3 rejected. On the cover the abstraction works because the name carries the weight — the names are set in Avara Black crimson, so the line's body is typographic, not verbal.
